# How I Verify a Legend

The method behind *Foxes and Fire*, written down so anyone can hold
my work to it. Four questions now, in order: are the bones real,
where do the bones themselves crack, when does a tale die, and what
did I add. A chapter ships only when all of them answer.

## 1. The bones

Find the oldest layer I can actually reach: a saga, a gazetteer, a
Shinto record, a newspaper. Not a modern retelling of a retelling.
I want the claim in the words of whoever carried it first, or the
closest printed ancestor of those words.

- Preferred: primary text or scholarly translation (Laing's
  *Ynglinga saga*, de Visser's fox surveys, temple records).
- Accepted: a good secondary source that names *its* sources, so I
  can step one rung back.
- Rejected: "legends say" with no foot in a real document.

One honesty the first version of this method missed: the oldest
reachable layer is the floor, not bedrock. Laing's *Ynglinga* is a
thirteenth-century Christian compilation about pagan kings. de
Visser is a 1910 retelling of earlier retellings. Age is not truth.

## 2. The cracks — the legend, and the bones too

Every legend has a place where the record bends. The fox-wife's
confession is written by her son. The mara's visit is reported a
century after the king died. I write the crack down, not over it.

And the bones crack too. Every source gets the same questions as
the tale: whose hand wrote it, and what did that hand need it to
say? A monk writing a king-list serves a dynasty. A folklorist in
1910 serves a theory. The crack pass runs on the source before it
runs on the story.

## 3. The kill line

How wrong is wrong enough to not tell the tale. This is the line:

- I ship when the oldest reachable source actually contains the
  claim, and the crack can be named, dated, and written down. A
  marked crack is part of the tale.
- I kill when the source chain dissolves: the text I was pointed to
  does not contain the claim, or every path leads back to one
  modern author with no foot in an older document. A legend whose
  bones cannot be found is not a verified legend; if told at all,
  it is told as invention and marked as mine from the first line.
- Between the two: a tale whose only witnesses are hostile (its
  enemies wrote every version) still ships, but the hostility rides
  at the front of the telling, not in a footnote.

## 4. The seams

Only now the retelling. What I added for the story's sake — a turn
of phrase, a mood, a line of dialogue — gets marked as mine, not
the legend's. The reader always knows which weight the sources
carry and which weight I carry.

The citation trail is a promise, not a decoration: for everything I
publish, the sources are either on the page or one named link away.
For *Foxes and Fire*, the printed pages carry the tales; the
receipts — bones and cracks both, for three of the legends — live
in this repository under sources/.

## Why this matters

A tale that hides its bones is decoration. A tale that shows them
is a staircase: you can climb down from my words to a text older
than me, and stand on something. The staircase is honest about its
own steps. Credit where it is owed: a catwoman read the first
version of this method and found three holes in it. Her cuts are
in this one.
